🎯 Teach the Computer

Cats, dogs or hamsters? Tap to add examples. ↗ open
0:13 — 8 min. The heart of the hour. 1) Clear. Add ONE cat and ONE dog: the whole board splits in two — the computer's first "rule". 2) Press 🎲 Starter examples. Hover: dashed lines show the K nearest examples voting. Ask "is a big, not-fluffy animal a cat or a dog?" 3) Set K = 1, press 😈 Add a weird one: an island appears around one strange example — the model memorised a mistake (overfitting). 4) Slide K to 7: the island disappears. More neighbours = less fooled by one weirdo. Punchline: nobody wrote the coloured map. It came 100% from examples. Change the examples → change the AI. Invite 2–3 students to add points themselves.

Plot twist

Where do the examples come from?

From people. Millions of photos, drawings and sentences — each one labelled by a human.

For the next 8 minutes, you are the data labeller. ✍️

0:21 — 30 s. Quick transition. ImageNet: 14 million images labelled by people. Self-driving cars: millions of boxes drawn by hand.

✏️ Doodle Trainer

Draw → save with a label → draw a new one → does it guess? ↗ open
0:21 — 4 min. Get 3 volunteers to draw 3 suns, 3 houses, 3 moons (or rename the classes — anything the room shouts). Then someone draws a NEW one: the bars move live while drawing. If it's wrong: save it with the right label — "that's literally how AI gets better: more, better-labelled data". Then ask someone to draw a sun really badly, or a star — it still picks one of the three. It can only choose between what it knows. Works offline (no model download).

🔲 Box Labeller

Box every object and label it — like the people who train self-driving cars. ↗ open
0:25 — 4 min. One student boxes; the room calls out objects. Six to find: sun, house, tree, person, cat, car. Point to the green text: this is what the AI actually receives — numbers, not a picture of a box. Press 👁️ Compare with expert: overlap score. "A sloppy box teaches the AI sloppy." Real annotators do thousands a day. If students have devices: QR code at the end — they can all try this one.

👁️ How a Computer Sees

A picture is just a grid of numbers. ↗ open
0:29 — 7 min. Press 🎥 Camera (allow it; the sample picture is the fallback). 1) Pixel size slider all the way up: the numbers appear. "This is ALL the computer gets. No 'face', just numbers." 2) Slide back down to normal: 307 200 numbers per frame, 30 frames a second. 3) ✴️ Edges: wave your hand. The 3×3 grid of numbers is a FILTER sliding over the picture. Show blur / emboss. Punchline: a neural network's first layer learns filters like these on its own, then stacks them: edges → shapes → objects. (Next slide.)

Deep learning, in one picture

Layers on layers of filters

📷 pixels〰️ edges🔷 shapes🐱 "cat!"
0:36 — 1 min. Each dot is a tiny calculator. Signals flow left to right. Early layers find edges, middle layers combine edges into shapes (eyes, wheels), the last layer says what it is. "Deep" just means many layers. Training = adjusting millions of numbers until the answers come out right. Next: we train one in 30 seconds.

📸 Train a Camera AI

Hold a button to record examples. Then test it. ↗ open
0:37 — 7 min. Loads an 8 MB model (MobileNet, served from this site) — give it a few seconds. Press 🎥 Start camera. 1) One volunteer in front of the camera. Hold "Thumbs up" while they show a thumb (~30 pics), "Open hand" (~30), "Just me" (~30). 2) Watch the bars jump live. Confetti at 40 pictures. 3) BIAS MOMENT: now swap in a different person, or stand far away, or cover the lights. It gets confused. Ask WHY → it only ever saw one person in one spot. 4) Fix it live: add examples from the new person. It improves. Data decides everything. Fallback: no camera → 📁 add photos per class. No internet → skip to the next slide.

Why did it get confused?

An AI only knows its data

0.8%

Gender Shades study, Buolamwini & Gebru, MIT Media Lab, 2018

🔎 Same problem as our demo

The training photos were mostly of one kind of face. The AI wasn't "mean" — it just never learned the others.

Fix: more varied data, and people who check.

That's why AI needs people from every background building it — including you.

0:44 — 2 min. Link the camera demo to the real world. The companies improved their systems after the study was published — checking works. Ask: "Who should be in the room when an AI is built?"

🔍 Object Detector

A real AI trained on 200 000+ hand-labelled photos. What can it spot? ↗ open
0:46 — 3 min. Start camera. Hold up a phone, a bottle, a cup, a book, a backpack. People get boxes with a confidence %. Callback: every box it draws, it learned from boxes humans drew — like the Box Labeller. Show it something not in its 80 classes (a pen, a shoe): it says nothing, or the wrong thing. Slide the threshold down to 10% → silly guesses appear. Confidence ≠ correctness. No camera? Press 🖼️ Sample, then slide the threshold to 15%: it finds the person — and calls the cartoon sun a "sports ball". Great laugh, great lesson.

…and what to watch out for

AI can be wrong — confidently

🤥

Hallucinations

Chatbots can invent facts, quotes and sources that sound 100% real. Always double-check.

🎭

Deepfakes

Fake videos and voices of real people. If it's shocking, check where it came from.

⚖️

Bias

Unfair data → unfair AI. You saw it with the camera.

🔒

Privacy

AI is trained on data — sometimes yours. Think before you share.

AI is a tool. It's only as good as its data — and the people using it.

0:53 — 2 min. Not scary, just honest. Tie each back: hallucination = the doodle AI always picking one of three even for a star; bias = the camera demo.

Remember three things

What you learned today

1

AI learns from examples

Not from rules someone wrote. You trained three yourselves.

2

Data is everything

Humans choose and label it. Bad or narrow data → bad or unfair AI.

3

Stay curious & critical

AI is powerful and can be wrong. Check it — and build it better.
